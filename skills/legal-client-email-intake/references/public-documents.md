# Public documents and personal-status continuity

From the firm's procedure "בדיקת תעודות ציבוריות". This governs what to notice
and what to ask for in a reply. It does not authorise stating a documentary
requirement as settled law: see references/source-verification.md, because the
procedure itself cites no authority for its requirements.

## Names across certificates

Names must be compared across every document in the file: birth certificate,
current passport, earlier passports, marriage certificate, divorce certificate,
personal-status certificate, police clearance, name-change certificates,
documents from earlier countries of residence.

Discrepancies to look for: maiden against married name, a surname changed on
marriage or divorce, a changed given name, different Latin spelling, a different
order of given and family names, a middle name present on one document and
absent from another, an abbreviated or omitted name, and a different
transliteration between countries.

Where a name differs, identify whether a bridging document is needed: a
name-change certificate, marriage or divorce certificate, an affidavit, an
official confirmation from the country of origin, or a written legal explanation
to the Population Authority.

## Personal-status continuity

Build a year-by-year timeline of the applicant's personal status and look for
unexplained gaps: birth, first marriage, divorce, death of a spouse, later
marriage, name change, passport issue, moves between countries, when the
relationship with the Israeli partner began, marriage to that partner, entry to
Israel, visa expiry, and the date status regularisation was applied for.

A gap between a divorce and a later marriage is not closed by the two
certificates alone. Someone divorced in 1995 who remarried in 2005 may also need
a personal-status certificate, a CENOMAR or equivalent, a confirmation of no
intervening marriage, a foreign population-registry confirmation or a consular
certificate covering 1995 to 2005. Never assume a past divorce is by itself
proof of capacity to marry years later.

## Authentication

For each certificate consider whether it is an original, whether it is recent
enough, whether the issuing state is a party to the Hague Convention, whether an
apostille is required, whether consular authentication is needed where an
apostille is unavailable, whether a notarised Hebrew translation is required,
whether the apostille is on the certificate itself rather than only on a
covering document, and whether the name on the apostille matches the name on the
certificate.

Treat with particular care: old certificates, certificates issued long before
the file was opened, certificates whose name does not match the passport, and
certificates that do not cover the whole period at issue.

## Countries of residence

Establish every country the applicant has lived in, not only the country of
citizenship or birth: where they were born, lived, worked, and held a work
permit, residence, residency or citizenship. Consider whether a police
clearance certificate is needed from each, whether the name on it matches the
birth certificate, passport and marriage certificates, and whether it must show
both maiden and married names. A fingerprint or passport number helps identify
the person but does not remove the need for matching names or a bridging
document.

## In a reply

Two limits apply.

A document read by machine from a scan is evidence of what the machine saw. Never
assert a name or date discrepancy from a machine-read document; ask the client to
confirm against the original, or say the original must be examined.

The firm's own thresholds are not yet recorded here: how long a stay makes a
police certificate necessary, and how recent a certificate must be. Do not invent
either. Ask, or leave the placeholder for the lawyer.
